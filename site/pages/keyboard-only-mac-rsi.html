Short answer

This isn't medical advice. If you have pain, numbness or tingling, see a doctor or physiotherapist. If you want to use the mouse or trackpad less, a Mac can do a lot from the keyboard: ⌘Space to open apps, Full Keyboard Access and Voice Control built in, and the shortcuts in every app's menus. For clicking things you can see, a label app like No Mouse Mode lets you press ⇧⌘Space and type a button's letters. Change one habit at a time; the one-week plan below shows how.

First: what this page is and isn't

We make a keyboard app, not medical advice. We won't tell you what causes wrist pain, whether a keyboard is better for you than a mouse, or what will make it go away, because we don't know your situation and it isn't our field. A doctor or physiotherapist can look at how you work, including your keyboard, chair, desk and breaks, and tell you what to change.

What we can do is explain, practically, how to run a Mac with less reaching for the pointer, if that's part of the plan you've settled on. Use as much or as little of it as you like.

Where mouse and trackpad use comes from

Notice what you reach for the pointer to do. For most people it's a short list:

  • opening and switching apps;
  • pressing buttons, links, tabs and menu items;
  • scrolling;
  • right-clicking for a menu;
  • dragging, selecting, and clicking a spot that isn't a button.

Each one has a keyboard answer. You don't have to replace all of them on day one.

Keyboard shortcuts: the easiest wins

Mac apps show keyboard shortcuts next to their menu items. Open a menu with Control-F2 (or Fn-Control-F2), look along the right-hand side, and pick the two or three you use most. Learning a shortcut for something you do twenty times a day is the cheapest change you can make, and it costs nothing to try.

If you use a keyboard app, check that it doesn't get in the way of these. In No Mouse Mode, any shortcut with Command, Control or Option (like ⌘S, ⌘W or Control-→) goes straight to your app, even while No Mouse Mode is on.

Clicking what you can see: No Mouse Mode

Shortcuts don't cover the button in the middle of a web page or the file in a Finder window. That's the gap No Mouse Mode fills. It's one of several apps that do this (see the best keyboard mouse apps for Mac), so compare before you buy.

Click labels

Press ⇧⌘Space. Every button, link, tab and menu in the front window, plus the menu bar and the Dock, gets a short letter label. Type it to click. Hold Shift with the last letter to right-click, Command for a Command-click (a new tab in browsers), or Option to double-click. Tab keeps the labels open for several clicks in a row. Labels for a window stay the same each time, so frequent clicks turn into muscle memory.

Type to click

Press ⇧⌘/ and type part of what a button says: case, spaces and accents don't matter, and first letters work ("nf" finds New Folder). Return clicks. Handy when you know the name but don't want to look for a label.

Hold-to-use with Right Command

If switching modes feels like one more thing to remember, turn on hold-to-use in Settings and choose Right Command (or Right Option). Hold it, use the mouse keys, let go and you're typing again. It's off by default; ⇧⌘M works either way.

Moving the pointer, scrolling and dragging

While it's on, W A S D move the pointer smoothly (hold Shift for fast, Q for one-pixel precise), Space clicks and held down drags, Return right-clicks, and the arrow keys scroll. ⇧⌘J numbers each scrollable pane so you can pick one and page through it. The jump grid (E) reaches anywhere on screen in two or three presses, in any app.

Saved spots

If you click the same place again and again, such as a send button or a toolbar in an app without labels, hold Shift and press 1–9 to save the pointer's position. Press the number to jump back there, and ` to return to where you were before the jump.

Practice

The first-run setup walks you through it, and the Practice window (in the menu bar icon) has nine hands-on lessons. Press H any time it's on to see every key. Every key and shortcut can be changed in Settings.

Honest limits: labels and type to click depend on apps describing their buttons to macOS, which most do. For apps that don't, an optional setting reads on-screen text with Apple's on-device text recognition (needs Screen Recording permission; nothing is saved or sent), and the grid and W A S D work everywhere. No Mouse Mode costs $19.99 one-time for 1 Mac (as of October 2026), with no free trial; the demo on this page uses the same keys. It runs on macOS 14 Sonoma or later, Apple silicon and Intel.

A gradual one-week plan

Going cold turkey tends to be frustrating. Keep the trackpad or mouse within reach all week and use it whenever you're stuck. The goal is to move one kind of task at a time. If anything is uncomfortable, stop and talk to your doctor or physio.

  1. Day 1: Open apps from the keyboard. Use ⌘Space and type the app's name. Don't change anything else.
  2. Day 2: Three shortcuts. Pick the three menu commands you use most and learn their shortcuts from the menus.
  3. Day 3: Click buttons without the pointer. Try click labels (⇧⌘Space) or Full Keyboard Access in one app you use a lot. If you have No Mouse Mode, run through a few Practice lessons first.
  4. Day 4: Scroll and right-click. Use the arrow keys to scroll and Return (or Shift with a label) to right-click. Try scroll areas (⇧⌘J) on a long page.
  5. Day 5: Type to click and hold-to-use. Try ⇧⌘/ for buttons you know by name. Turn on hold-to-use with Right Command if toggling feels clumsy.
  6. Day 6: Your awkward spots. Save a spot (Shift+number) for anything you click often that has no label, and use the jump grid (E) for the rest.
  7. Day 7: Review. Note which tasks still send your hand to the trackpad. Keep what helped, drop what didn't, and change any key that feels awkward in Settings.
